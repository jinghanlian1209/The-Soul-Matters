Life Happened

For October

On growing up, missing home, and making it into the forever we promised as children.

November 2, 2026Los Angeles9 min read

Mom texted to say she wasn’t feeling well again. I asked a friend to help her get an appointment with a specialist, and suddenly it all felt unfamiliar. My parents used to be the ones asking their friends for help. Now my friends and I have somehow reached the age when we can take things into our own hands.

On the phone, I reminded him how I used to tease him when we were little: imagine you actually becoming a doctor. Imagine me opening the door to a consulting room and finding you sitting there, waiting to examine me. I would never stop laughing. Who knew the boy would actually come in handy one day?

I know Mom isn’t well, but I’m afraid to ask too much. What I’m ashamed of is that I think about her every day, yet somehow it has been so long since I made time to call. I know a call will take a while. There is the time difference to work around. She will ask about school, my health, work, my love life, and I will have to summon an enormous amount of energy to explain all of it. This whole tangled mess.

And beyond worrying, there may be little she can do. I know she will say, “Go as far as you can. Don’t worry about Mom.” I also know she will say, “And if you go too far, if you get too tired, come home. I’m here.”

I envy everyone who can see their parents without much trouble. Even the ones who get to argue with them. I envy that, too.

After an interview the other day, I discussed salary with HR. I know how greedy and changeable people can be, and I am no exception. Before this opportunity appeared, I kept saying that all I wanted was a chance. Anything would do. I thought, rather innocently, that I simply wanted to stay and explore a bigger world, that money might not be what mattered most to me.

But the moment I heard the offer, I wavered. It wasn’t even low. I just greedily felt that my intelligence and abilities should earn me something better. And suddenly I was lost. I no longer knew what I wanted.

My closest friends. The parents I love most. Better pay and benefits. Everything familiar. All of it would be within easy reach if I went back to China. I don’t know whether there is something here worth this much sacrifice. I don’t know whether the things I will spend my whole life searching for were already mine at the beginning. I don’t know whether I have given up too much.

That evening, I walked past the athletic field. The soccer team was practicing, voices carrying across the grass. The evening breeze brushed past me, and a round moon hung overhead. For a moment, I lost my bearings. Was this Xi’an? Hangzhou? America?

It felt like the walk home after school. Like the ride from the lecture halls to my undergraduate dorm on my electric scooter.

So this is how far I have come from home. One step, then another.

Have I really grown up? I ask myself.

Have I really changed?

This month, I saw some very dear friends. Whenever I’m with them, I feel as though I have never changed at all. I’ll do something completely uninhibited, then catch myself thinking: how am I still such a childish little kid?

The truth is, I know how badly I need anchors in this unfamiliar place. I need moments that let me feel I belong. I know that my reliance on Daniel, on those close friends, carries something of my reliance on home, on my parents. I need them so much. They let me catch my breath in this strange adult world. They let me be a child.

I still remember watching the distance between Daniel’s location and mine shrink from 3,152 kilometers. I watched his car make its way up from the southern end of Lake Michigan, closer and closer, until the distance became zero.

My heart was racing with excitement. Yet when he was finally standing in front of me, it felt so familiar that we might have seen each other yesterday.

“I bought a new CD,” he said. “We can listen as soon as you get in.”

“Whose album?”

He smiled. “Olivia Rodrigo. I know you’ve been listening to her lately.”

In the car, I marveled at how quickly time had passed.

“We’ve known each other for ten years already.”

“Twelve,” he said, keeping his eyes on the road.

One word, landing so lightly and so heavily on my heart.

That night, after we’d been drinking, I asked him to throw away the trash. As I handed him a bottle cap, I hesitated. Should I keep it? Then I thought, come on, you’re a grown woman. Be a little more mature. Keeping a bottle cap felt a bit too sentimental, a bit too much. So I told him to throw it away with everything else.

Once we were back in the car, he smiled and opened his hand. There was the bottle cap.

“You didn’t throw it away?”

“Keep it as a souvenir,” he said. “I knew you wanted to.”

I looked at him. In that moment, I felt so far from home, and so close to it.

We have come so far. We still have so far to go. But what a lucky thing, really: we have made it into the forever we promised each other as children.

Forever has no endpoint. Perhaps this very moment is what forever looks like.

We once sat in a tiny classroom, imagining that we would be friends for life. Even if we ended up at opposite ends of the earth, we would still see each other often.

And somehow, we have actually done it.

Like the “many years later” scene in a movie.

We are still young. We are still chasing new dreams, still climbing toward new summits. But we are still here, beside each other. Isn’t that already a happy ending?

I am lucky to have these happy endings. I am also, in a way, trapped by them. They make me expect every relationship, every friendship and every love, to end beautifully.

The other day, Hazel accused me of being warm on the outside and cold underneath. Whenever we meet, she said, I’m so enthusiastic, but once we’re apart, I barely even reply to messages. I have too many friends. I’m like this with everyone.

I told her that my energy is so limited. Making friends does come easily to me, but I know very well that I cannot keep everyone in my life. Once I decide I want you to stay, though, I will do everything I can.

I know it can be frightening when I begin to expect things from someone. Expectations and sincerity can be heavy things to carry. That warmth on the surface, that distance underneath, protects me. It protects you, too. But come a little closer, and you might discover that “warm” is nowhere near enough to describe my heart. Scalding, perhaps.

It causes me so much pain. I feel too intensely. I hope for too much. Perhaps I have acquired many of an adult’s abilities while keeping a heart that is still far too innocent, far too young.

I am always thinking in lifetimes.

I ask too much. I expect too much. I expect people to show up for me the way these friends of more than a decade always have.

Sometimes it is exhausting to live in the adult world with a child’s heart. The world is so big. There are so many people. I so often feel that I cannot manage it all. I don’t know whether I should keep expecting things from people. But so often, I cannot make myself stop.

It hurts so much.

I asked Daniel whether I really hadn’t grown up.

He said that growing up and changing are different things. I have grown up, he said. I just haven’t changed. I have gained the abilities of an adult without losing the heart I started with.

He said he thought that was wonderful. He hoped I would always keep that heart. That I would always have things to look forward to, in people and in the world.

I came across a post about the sadness that arrives at the height of happiness: you have just spent the most beautiful evening with your friends, and suddenly you realize it is about to end. Over the years to come, you will return to this moment in your mind countless times. But you will never actually be able to return.

I tagged Daniel underneath it, with a crying face.

He didn’t reply. He just called and asked when we were booking our tickets to New Orleans.

I burst out laughing. All that feeling-type melancholy, caught so steadily by a thinking type and turned into an actual plan to see each other.

“Instead of thinking so much, let’s make the next visit happen,” he said. “The world is so big, and look how far we’ve already come. We’re already doing so, so well, you know? Don’t think too much. Sleep properly. Eat properly.”

I suddenly thought of going to Boston to see Q in the spring. We hadn’t seen each other in eight years. For almost three days and nights, we barely slept. We just talked.

On the day I left, he gave me a seven-page letter. At the end, he wrote:

“Don’t let it be eight years again. And the smallest thing of all, which turned out to be all that: thank you for being glad to see me.”

I have carried those words with me.

And little by little, I have begun to adjust. Less overthinking. If I want to do something, do it. If I want to see someone, go. If I want to love someone, love them.

I like this version of myself. I like being able to act without weighing every possible consequence. I like that courage.

When I miss you, I will come to see you whenever I can. When I love you, I will love you with everything I have.

If I cannot be someone who takes everything lightly, then let me be someone who feels deeply and follows through. Perhaps that way, I will collect a hundred times as many moments of happiness.

This has become such a long essay. I started writing it last week, and today I have finally found something like an ending.

For October.

May November bring a few new things to understand, and many more moments of happiness.

A line I want to keep

“Forever has no endpoint. Perhaps this very moment is what forever looks like.”

Made with too many thoughts and just enough courage.
